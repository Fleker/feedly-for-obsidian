Why ‘What’s Opera, Doc?’ Looks Like That - Animation Obsessive

Classic status wasn’t exactly the team’s goal. This was, in some ways, a workaday Bugs Bunny production. “What’s Opera, Doc? is one many Europeans adore,” Chuck Jones said in the ‘70s. “They call that my ‘masterpiece,’ which it may or may not be; I didn’t think about it at the time.”
He did know it was a bit ambitious. At a cost of $35,510 (almost $430,000 today), it was spendier than most Warner cartoons. It took seven weeks compared to the standard five. To get the extra work done, Jones and his crew secretly shaved a couple weeks off other, easier films and faked their time cards. It was something they did on occasion. (The mysterious price increases angered the managers, Jones said, but they were “not very bright” and didn’t figure out the trick.)

Here's a minor art mystery. I wasn't able to find information on the source or artist behind this illustration of a close encounter of the third kind. There's even a signature on the bottom-center of the image.

Commenters on Tumblr said the name might be something similar to "J. Farre" or "L. Fere," and one says the image resembles an "artist's rendition of witness accounts for the 'Cape Girardeau' UFO crash."

Commenters on Twitter had a lot of utterly garbage guesses that all appeared to be AI-generated and which I won't bother to repeat here.

However, less than an hour after I posted it on Bluesky, one lurker had the best guess yet: It appears to be the signature of an artist named Julio Freire, an Argentine illustrator and cartoonist who freelanced for a lot of Argentine publications in the '70s and '80s. And speaking of solving art mysteries, here's a cool one: This 1980 cover to Mattel’s Dungeons & Dragons Computer Labyrinth went uncredited for decades.

Now, thanks to a posthumously-released Feb 2026 interview on the Sighs and Whispers podcast between historian Laura McLaws Helms and illustrator and artist Richard Ellescas, we know that Ellescas is the artist behind it.
